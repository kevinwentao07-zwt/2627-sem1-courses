Student Activity 3: One- and Two-way Communication 
 
Learning objectives:  
1. To experience how two-way communication works through recurrent mutual transmission of 
messages and feedback; 
2. To tell the differences between one-way and two-way communication 
Materials: 
Drawings, paper, pens (one piece of paper and one pen per student) 
Procedure: 
Part 1: One-way communication  
1. Put up the following drawing (or something similar) on the board and cover it up. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
2. Students sit in two lines, back to back. 
3. One line of students (Group A) face the drawing, the other line (Group B) sits with their back 
facing the drawing. 
4. Uncover the drawing.  Group A students describe the details of the drawing to Group B 
students.  Group B students listen to Group A students’ description to draw the details as 
accurately as possible. 
5. Only Group A students can talk and describe the drawing; Group B students cannot talk or ask 
any questions. 
6. Stop after 5 mins for everybody to see the products of the drawing.

2. This time, Group B students responsible for drawing can ask questions about the drawing; 
Group A students can clarify and respond on the spot. 
3. Stop after 5 mins.  Compare the products of the drawing in Parts 1 and 2 to see which one likes 
more like the original. 
Total duration: 20 mins (including the sharing/discussion after the two activities) 
Conclusion: 
One-way communication can lead to a lot of misunderstanding during the coding and decoding 
process.  Two-way communication enables both the senders and the receivers to grasp the meaning 
intended. 
In daily life, we often respond to messages with ‘ok’, ‘got it’, ‘understood’ etc. to acknowledge 
receipt.  We may also ask questions to ensure we get the correct message.  This turns the sender 
into the receiver who will respond.  This recurrent process enables mutual understanding. 
Although two-way communication is effective, one-way communication has its advantages.  For 
example, when the purpose is to give command for the receivers to take actions, when there is no 
room for negotiation, and when the receivers must follow the instructions, one-way 
communication can lead to prompt and effective actions, as in the army.
