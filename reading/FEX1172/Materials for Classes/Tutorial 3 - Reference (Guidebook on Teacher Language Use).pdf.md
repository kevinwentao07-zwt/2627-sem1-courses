1. INTRODUCTION 
 
Language is an important tool for exchanging ideas, transmitting messages and facilitating mutual 
understanding among people. The Taizu (Grand Reunion) Chapter of the Book of Huainanzi has this saying: 
“We use language to express our thoughts and emotions. We listen to enable us to understand others’ 
thoughts and emotions.” From this, we can see the importance of language use for human communication 
purposes.   
A teacher’s interpersonal and presentation skills are closely related to students’ achievement, attendance, 
self-image, attitude towards the school and their behaviour at school. There is a saying: “A kind word keeps 
you warm in winter, but a bad one makes you cold even in June.” One word from the teacher can change 
the life of a student. As professionals, teachers must be cautious with what they say. Think twice before 
you say something that may be hurting. Keep your mouth shut when you are angry. Even when you want 
to be humorous, you have to consider how students may feel. All in all, teachers should exercise wisdom in 
using language. Your appropriate language and behaviour can enhance students’ intellectual ability, 
cultivate their good behaviour and help them establish congenial interpersonal relationships. These are 
the sacred duties of a teacher and the keen expectations from society at large. 
Having received years of rigorous teacher training, you still have to face a lot of challenges when you 
become a novice teacher and a role models for your students. We hope that this booklet can provide you 
with some hints on teacher language use to help you quickly adjust to your new identity as a teacher and 
start committing yourself to nurturing the next generation. However, you must all bear in mind that 
appropriate and effective language use alone cannot earn you the respect of a professional teacher. You 
also need to make persistent effort to keep abreast of new knowledge in your field and in general, and to 
continually develop your own attributes and virtues to be a good role model and company for your 
students and others.

2. TEACHER AND TEACHER LANGUAGE  
 
Ever since ancient times, classroom instruction has always been done through word of mouth. In Confucius 
times, language was used to “propagate the doctrine, impart professional knowledge and resolve doubts”. 
More importantly, a teacher’s language use has subtle influence on students’ language acquisition. Hence 
the importance of teacher language use is beyond doubt. 
Taiwan scholar, Dr. Wang Shu-li (1997) compares the importance of interpersonal relationship and 
communication skills with the ability of a surgeon in mastering the surgical tools. This analogy is 
appropriate because teachers have to work with a lot of people, including students, parents and colleagues 
and they spend a lot of time on communication and presentation every day. To win the trust of parents and 
students and the respect of the community, teachers should therefore demonstrate their expertise and 
refinement in language use and speak discreetly as a professional. 
There are three main aspects to consider in relation to the purpose of teacher language use: 
1. 
To teach and to establish the teacher’s professional image; 
2. 
To facilitate students’ learning and development of a positive self-image; 
3. 
To build interpersonal relationship. 
The above will be elaborated in the succeeding chapters.

expected by and acceptable to society?  
To meet public expectations, a teacher should exhibit the following qualities: 
1. 
Takes responsibility for passing on and further cultivating the inherited civilization; very 
knowledgeable and well-learnt. 
2. 
Has exemplary professional knowledge and skills (presentation skills in particular); can co-construct 
knowledge with students. 
3. 
Shows enthusiasm in teaching and concern for student growth.  
4. 
Demonstrates noble virtues and acts as a role model for students.

The beauty of the language used by professional teachers can be appreciated in terms of the following 
functions (Liu Bo-kui, Wang Yan, Duan Bian-xia, 2000) 
 
Prescriptive: right volume, appropriate pace, varied rhythm (not monotone), precise use of 
words, accurate grammar, simple and easy to understand.  
Clearly heard and easily understood; pleasing to the ear. 
 
Scientific: Coherently and logically presented with evidence; objective. 
- For example: The teacher said in the class, “The great scientist Albert Einstein believed in God, 
so it proves that God exists!” This assumption is unscientific and illogical, and will not lead to 
understanding. 
 
Educational: Caring and inspiring, teach students according to their aptitude, cultivate positive 
values and character, foster intellectual curiosity and the love for learning. 
 
Aesthetic: Appropriate use of rhetorical skills such as metaphor, personification, exaggeration 
and analogy so as to motivate learning and make the lessons lively and interesting.  
 
Appealing: Appeals to reason as well as emotion, pleasing tone coupled with the appropriate 
body language (eye contact and hand gestures), tone and attitude. 
 
Adaptable: Can respond promptly and adapt flexibly the content of the conversation according 
to changes in time, place and people.

4. FACILITATING STUDENTS' LEARNING AND DEVELOPMENT OF A POSITIVE SELF-IMAGE  
 
According to the Teacher Education Department of the National Education Committee (1994), a teacher’s 
language can be divided into two main categories: classroom language and interpersonal language. 
How do we make use of classroom language to facilitate student learning? 
Corresponding to the stages in a lesson, classroom language can be divided into five categories: opening, 
lead-in, teaching/explaining, questioning and evaluation language. (Tu Xiu-ru, Wu Cai-wei, Xu Ai-ling, 1989) 
  
1. 
The opening and the lead-in are of paramount importance to a lesson. There is the saying “Well begun, 
half done”. A teacher should aim to excite students’ curiosity and arouse their interest before leading 
them into the subject matter.  Teachers can consider using stories, anecdotes, daily life experiences 
or questions to start the lesson, bearing in mind that they should be presented in the aesthetic and 
appealing ways (see section “How do we help students develop a positive self-concept image 
through the use of interpersonal language?”) so as to motivate students to learn. 
2. 
Teaching/Explaining language takes up the majority part of a lesson. Teachers are expected to use 
the prescriptive, scientific and educational functions of the language objectively and effectively. If 
they can strengthen this language use with the aesthetic and appealing functions, then their teaching 
efficiency will be further enhanced and students will be more motivated to learn. 
3. 
Questioning is effective for both teaching and assessment. Not only can it enhance teacher-student 
and student-student interaction, it can also encourage students to participate and think more while 
allowing the teacher to see how much of the teaching content the students have grasped. Appropriate 
questioning can inspire students. However, when done inappropriately, students will lose interest in 
learning, lose confidence in expressing themselves, or even feel hurt. 
Questioning that creates an effective learning environment (Liu Xian-guo, 2000): 
 
Smiling Questioning: Asking questions with a smiling face and a caring tone can help reduce 
anxiety and allow students to express themselves in a relaxing environment. 
 
Situational Questioning: When students find it hard to grasp abstract concepts, the teacher can 
create a similar situation through stories, fables and experiments etc. to help them understand 
the concepts more clearly. 
 
General Questioning: Teachers should make an effort to give equal opportunities for all students 
to answer questions in class instead of focusing on a small group. This can help to engage the 
whole class in active thinking and the learning atmosphere will be greatly enhanced. 
 
Visual Questioning: Use gestures, real objects, props or graphics to help students understand 
the question.

 
Interest Questioning: Use skeptical thinking methods (to arouse students’ curiosity), 
brainstorming, stories, games or competitions to complement the questions so as to stimulate 
students to explore and make positive responses. 
 
Reward Questioning: Verbal praises, small gifts or group marks can motivate students to 
respond with enthusiasm. [N.B.: Verbal praises and helping students to gain a sense of success are 
a much better choice than tangible gifts because the former encourages intrinsic motivation 
whereas the latter only encourages extrinsic motivation; students will not be interested in small 
gifts or group marks anymore when they are in senior levels.]   
 
Progressive Questioning: There are different levels of questions: simple to challenging, concrete 
to abstract, factual to conceptual. Use a series of questions to support in-depth learning: Start with 
simple questions and gradually move on to more challenging ones. Start with concrete questions 
and gradually proceed to abstract questions. Start with “what” questions and gradually 
proceeding to “why” and “how” questions. 
- e.g. When the teacher is showing a credit card to the class, s/he can ask: “What is this?” then 
“Why do we need a credit card?” then “How is this used?”  
Other than this, teachers can also encourage students to be skeptical and critical. This should be done 
at the right time and with the right person. The most crucial is to encourage students to speak up and 
then you have to listen patiently for their answers. Interruption is not advisable. Praises should be 
given when a correct answer is made. Also, encourage students to supplement or revise their answer 
if it is incorrect. When a number of students have expressed their views on the same question, the 
teacher should do a summing up so as to help students reorganize and consolidate their knowledge. 
Often when a teacher asks questions, few students would like to answer in front of the whole class 
often because they are shy or they do not want to make a fool of themselves.  One way to increase 
student engagement effectively is to ask students to tell their partner their answer/thinking.  This 
enables all students to be engaged.  Then you can ask some students for their partner’s 
answer/thinking.  
4. 
Feedback from the teacher can help students grasp the key to learning, consolidate what they have 
learnt and enhance their motivation. This is like an archer shooting an arrow. He must understand 
the reason why the arrow deviates from the target so that he can do better the next time.  
      
     Feedback can have positive effects if it is done in the following ways (Liu Xian-guo, 2000): 
 
It must be precise: In a tactful and comforting way, the teacher clearly and precisely points out 
the error. 
- 
If the teacher only gives vague remarks such as: “Average performance. Some good and 
some bad. No surprises”, how can students know how to improve? The teacher should give

 
It must be timely: Feedback must be given at the right time. Given too early, it will interrupt



Example of a negative remark: “A lazy person. Makes no attempt for improvement.” 
- 
Example of a more positive remark: “Have not exerted your potential to the full. With 
greater effort, improvement will be made.” 
 
How do we help students develop a positive self-image through interpersonal language? 
 
To cater for students’ different needs, interpersonal language has the following five major functions:  To 
motivate, to suggest, to praise, to criticize and to converse. (Tu Xiu-ru, Wu Cai-wei, Xu Ai-ling, 1989) 
1. Language to motivate: Professional teachers are proficient with the use of motivating language to 
inspire students to learn, and enable them to discover their strengths and abilities so that they will 
learn with pleasure.  (Li Bor, no date) 
2. Language to suggest: To correct students’ misbehavior, very often it works better if done indirectly 
and positively because students will learn to do right under a congenial and relaxing atmosphere. 
This approach is far better than lecturing or coercion. 
Example: Students fight for seats in a public bus and refuse to give seats. 
 
The teacher reprimanded them directly: “How did the school teach you? You are so rude and 
I’m very disappointed with you!” 
 
The teacher talked to them gently, using implicit language: “When I was travelling in the 
subway in Taiwan last year, two students got up together and gave their seats to me. I was 
really touched!”  
3. Language to praise: Praises should be given according to the uniqueness and character of the 
student. Praises given in the form of public recognition can achieve very desirable results. However, 
praises must be done appropriately. Excessive or superfluous praises will do more harm than good.

the teacher-student relationship. Therefore, teachers must be very cautious when they criticize 
students. It is best done with a gentle demeanor so that students can feel their love and care. When 
reasoning with students, be careful with the words used to avoid hurting them. Students will find it 
more acceptable if criticisms are followed by constructive advice. 
5. Language to converse: When communicating with students, teachers should listen actively and 
have appropriate interaction so that students will be willing to disclose their feelings out of trust.

How do we talk to students? 
In the last chapter, we discussed how the teacher can make use of classroom language to facilitate learning 
and cited examples of classroom language use for the whole class or small groups. However, as teachers, 
we also play the additional role of counseling and discipline masters. In this respect, we often have to meet 
students individually and talk with them. Here are some key points about talking to students individually. 
 
Things to note when talking to students individually: 
1. Clear objectives: Be very clear about the objectives and expected outcome. This can avoid the talk 
drifting off to nowhere or you not knowing how to react to unpredicted emotional responses. 
2. Well-prepared: Prior to the talk, be fully informed of the student’s background e.g. academic 
record, personality, psychological status, family background, relationship with peers, how the 
incident happened etc. 
3. Well-planned: Is the time and place suitable? Is it too rash? Is the venue appropriate for private 
conversation? Is there privacy? What about the physical/psychological status of both parties? Will 
physical exhaustion or emotional instability affect the interview? If the two parties are of a different 
sex, it is imperative that no physical contact should be made. In such instances, is a one-to-one talk 
advisable? Is the meeting venue too secretive? Are there enough backup or contingency measures 
should the student become emotionally unstable?

4. Attitude: The talk should take place under an open, sincere and trusting atmosphere. The attitude 
of the teacher should be warm, respectful, sincere, encouraging, accepting, caring, supportive and 
positive. This kind of embracive attitude does not mean that the teacher condones what the student 
did but it brings assurance that the teacher will continue to love and support him however bad the 
situation is. This is based on the assumption that, like everybody else, the student is not perfect. The 
teacher’s role is to recognize him/his hard work and encourage him to make consistent effort for 
further improvement. Teachers should always try to understand their students and guide them 
towards positive ways of thinking. 
5. Language: Talk positively and objectively. When making comments, stay away from provocative or 
emotional language. One advice is to use “we” in place of “you” or “me” to convey the message that 
the teacher is willing to go through the difficult period with the student. 
 Undesirable ways of talk: condescending, vague and empty, vulgar, cynical, snitching, 
threatening or confrontational. 
 Language that would ruin relationship: mean, judgmental, domineering, uncompromising, 
subjective and sarcastic etc. 
 
6. Talk in progress: 
i. 
Opening: It serves as a warm-up to relieve anxiety and lower the defense mechanism so that 
the talk would proceed smoothly. The conversation can start by sending personal regards, 
for example: “After a day at school, are you tired?” Or you can choose a more casual topic, for 
example: “The song you chose for the Inter House Singing Contest last week was a nice one. 
You sang really well!” 
ii. 
Leading-in: Explain briefly the objective of the talk. For instance: “Peter, you used to be very 
attentive and proactive in class. But recently, I observed that you …… Are you in any trouble? 
I asked you to come because I wanted to know how I could help you.” 
iii. 
Listening: Listen with attention and patience; encourage frank talk by eye contact or short 
responses. Do not interrupt unless necessary. 
iv. 
Feedback: Give timely feedback 
 
Respond positively where possible. For example: “I appreciate your self-reflection 
and the effort you made in making improvement …” 
 
Show your understanding and acceptance. For example: “I know it was not easy to 
control your temper under that situation, but ….” 
 
Critical remarks and reminders should be objective and sensible, given in a gentle and 
sincere tone. Be precise and clear. Do not keep reiterating or bringing up unpleasant 
events from the past.

that s/he wants to commit suicide, or she is pregnant, you should not handle the case alone. Report to the 
school for advice, seek external help or make referral (but tell the student this is what you are going to do 
and why before you do it; students’ well-being is of utmost importance).  
There are situations where teachers face the dilemma of whether absolute confidentiality should be kept.  
When such situations arise, extra caution should be taken. Seek advice from your seniors or professional 
counselors before you make a sensible and professional decision.

“Danger Zone” when establishing relationship with students 
Student-teacher relationship is an important part of school education. An appropriate and positive 
relationship can motivate students to think positively, work hard and respect the teacher as a role model. 
However, if the teacher behaves inappropriately, it will not only damage the relationship but it can also 
tarnish the image of the teacher, the result of which can be detrimental. Teachers should therefore be alert 
and avoid falling into the “danger zone”, which can make themselves the centre of controversies and 
gossips attack. 
Here are some examples. 
 
Misunderstanding “Teacher and Friend” 
1. Using the same language 
Some teachers think that they should use the same language the students use in order that they can 
“mix better” with the students. Some may even speak foul language with students. Some may think 
that slangs are harmless. This actually results in the loss of teacher professionalism and can end up 
making oneself a laughing stalk when they do not even know the meaning and the origin of the slang. 
2. Acting too intimately 
Some teachers treat students as buddies and have intimate contact with them, such as hugging or 
embracing. Such intimacy is highly undesirable, especially male teachers and female students and, 
in the world today, between female teachers and male students. Also, they should be cautioned that 
once their relationship gets sour, what was regarded as acceptable will become repulsive.

3. Calling by nickname 
Students often call each other by their nicknames. Used among close friends, nicknames can be 
affectionate. However, when used maliciously or unkindly, it would damage the relationship. Hence 
teachers should think twice before they consider using nicknames in relating with students. 
 
In Chinese, being “teacher and friend” does imply the priority that “teacher” comes before “friend”. The 
idea is for the teacher to stoop down to the level of the students and relate to them on an equal level. There 
are also many touching stories of a former teacher becoming a life-long friend. (But that should be after the 
students have graduated from the school.) 
In English, the words ‘friend’ and ‘friendly’ can be a useful guide.  You can be friendly with students but you 
cannot be a friend to your students while you are a teacher in the school and they are students in the same 
school.  You can be friends with (some) students after they graduated / left the school. 
Very often, the impact of what you say not only depends on the content or the way of expression but it also 
depends on the relationship between the speaker and the receiver. If there is mutual trust and respect 
between the teacher and the students, then the effectiveness of teaching will be greatly enhanced. Thus, a 
high-effective teacher should be able to establish good relationship with their students. Nonetheless, 
sometimes it may be difficult to draw a line between “teacher” and “friend”. The basic principle to follow is 
a distinction between being a friend and being friendly. Below are some examples of the “danger zone” for

5. ESTABLISHING INTERPERSONAL RELATIONSHIP  
 
Teachers are regarded as “Engineers of the Human Soul” because every day they are dealing with people – 
students, colleagues, parents and even community members. So they must have good communication skills 
and be able to establish positive relationships with stakeholders through their language use. 
To establish good interpersonal relationships, the following are important points to note: 
   Respect and understanding 
   Acceptance and tolerance 
   Praise and encouragement 
   Recognition and appreciation 
   Empathy,  attention to what they may say 
   Listen attentively, give feedback 
   Be objective, do not take things personally 
 Adjust communication style to suit individual needs 
 No personal attack or total rejection 
 Do not criticize, do not blame, do not complain, do not attack, do not preach, do not despise 
 No vicious remarks or abusive words  
 Do not communicate when emotionally upset 
 Avoid breaching discrimination ordinance and violating professional conduct (Chinese/English) 
As the main tool for communication, language is of course crucial. However, very often we can pick up a lot 
of cues just by looking at the speaker, so non-verbal communication is of equal importance. Sometimes a 
look, a small gesture or a message conveyed through the tone and the body language can tell more than 
words. Thus, when trying to connect with people, we must also pay attention to our non-verbal behavior.

How do we talk to parents? 
 
Talking to parents is basically very similar to talking to students. However, since parents are adults with 
diverse backgrounds, and teacher-parent relationship is different from teacher-student relationship, the 
following points should be noted: 
1. Preparation is needed to understand the parents’ education level, profession, marital status, 
relationship with their children and their ways of parenting. This can help avoid embarrassing topics 
and formulate interaction strategies. 
2. Apart from the talk itself, it is natural that the parents may want to know more about their children’s 
life at school. Some may want to express their views on the school policy. So if time permits, the teacher 
should set aside some time for areas of common concern. 
3. Treat parents with equality and respect. Whatever the parents’ socio-economic status may be, teachers 
should always maintain their own professional integrity and graciousness. Their attitude should be 
sincere, open and considerate. 
4. Even though views may contradict, teachers should help the parents understand that the school has the 
commitment to solving the problems concerned. 
5. It is important the parents understand that the teacher is on the same line with the school so that they 
would not feel that the school has internal conflict. Assure them that the home and the school have 
shared interest as well as responsibilities in fostering the healthy growth of the students. 
6. If both parties have reached certain consensus or follow-up action during the talk, further contacts 
should be made to strengthen communication and report on the progress. 
7. Be open. Welcome parents to contact the appropriate school personnel whenever there is a need.

6. CONCLUSION 
 
A great teacher is a tutor of knowledge as well as a mentor for life. Your love for children will naturally lead 
you to think about how to help these children grow and thus how you should behave in order to be a good 
role model for them. A great teacher understands the power of language, so you always think before you 
speak and will use language cautiously. A great teacher has a passion for teaching. You will make sustained 
effort to enhance your language ability so that you can excel in teaching and your students will love to learn 
from you.  
 
What the hearts thinks the tongue speaks. Your language should reflect your prudence as a teacher. 
Prudent behaviour makes people feel you are reliable and trustworthy. This quality comes from within and 
is nurtured over time. It becomes a life-long commitment and a habit. Your passion for teaching and love 
for children will earn you respect from your students as they will look up to you for your professionalism 
as well as your genuineness as a life mentor. 
Language is a double-edged sword and this booklet is like a sword manual. If you study it seriously, practice 
diligently and make reflections constantly, then you will be able to master the secrets and become a sword 
master.
