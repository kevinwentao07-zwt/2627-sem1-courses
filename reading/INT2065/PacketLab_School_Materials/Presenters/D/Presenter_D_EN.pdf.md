Presenter D: Guided work, Q5 and the 
marking guide
25-minute presentation   /   Slides 30–34   /   15:00–17:25
Slide 30. Q1–Q4: guided work   15:00–15:35
Stage cue (do not read): Explain what each guided question reveals to the teacher.
The first four questions help us decide what to do while we are teaching. Q1 shows whether students 
can distinguish device functions. Q2 shows whether they see the boundary between two networks. In 
Q3, we compare the initial prediction with the recorded result and the reason they give. Q4 shows 
whether they distinguish forwarding, arrival order and reassembly. I would look at those answers 
while students work, then ask a prompt about the specific idea they have missed.
Slide 31. Q5: independent explanation   15:35–15:54
Stage cue (do not read): State what gets covered or collected and why.
Q5 checks what each student can explain independently. We cover or collect worksheet pages one 
to three and the notes, hide the website, and ask students to work without their partners. The teacher 
can clarify the instructions without supplying the answer.
Slide 32. Worksheet Q5: Exit task   15:54–16:05
Stage cue (do not read): Point to the instruction and four answer spaces.
Each student writes four points on this separate Q5 sheet. We collect it before showing the model 
answer.
Slide 33. What earns credit in Q5   16:05–17:02
Stage cue (do not read): Go through the four criteria once. Use a brief sample error to demonstrate the judgment.
Each distinct point earns one mark. We look for TCP splitting the data and tracking order, IP 
addressing and delivery, TCP checking for missing data and arranging retransmission, and TCP at 
the receiver reassembling the complete data in order. We accept different wording if the meaning is 
right. Suppose a student describes delivery correctly but says a switch puts the data back in order. 
We can credit the delivery point if it is clear, but the reassembly point is still missing. That tells us 
more about the next teaching step than a total on its own.
Slide 34. Marking guide: Q5   17:02–17:25
Stage cue (do not read): Point to the model answer as a teacher resource. Explain when the class sees it.
This is the teacher's marking guide. We keep it out of sight while students answer. After collecting 
Q5, we use the faster-switch question as a quick oral check. Then we show the model answer and 
ask students to add one correction in another colour, leaving the original visible.

Presenter D: Feedback, a fresh check and 
follow-up
25-minute presentation   /   Slides 35–37   /   17:25–20:00
Slide 35. Feedback, then a fresh check   17:25–18:18
Stage cue (do not read): Use the same-LAN misconception. Distinguish feedback from the next independent check.
Here is how I would act on a route misconception. A student says, “Every transfer has to go through 
a router.” I would go back to the wired PC, switch and local server, and ask the student to show 
where that particular route crosses a network boundary. It does not. If several students make the 
same mistake, I would reteach the comparison with the class. If it is a small group, I would use the 
relevant Follow-up A activity with them. Then I would remove the notes and give them a different 
case, so the new answer tells us more than a copied correction.
Slide 36. Progress check: A fresh case   18:18–18:45
Stage cue (do not read): Point to Progress Check Task 3 and state the expected explanation.
In this new case, the PC and server share a working same-LAN switch path. The AP is off on a 
separate laptop path. Students answer without notes: the wired PC needs neither that AP nor a 
router for this transfer. We are looking for the reason. This Progress Check is unmarked and helps 
the teacher decide whether to move on.
Slide 37. Support and extension   18:45–20:00
Stage cue (do not read): Contrast support and extension using concrete examples. Hand over to E.
During guided practice, we can simplify the route view, supply a word bank or let a student rehearse 
an explanation aloud. The student still has to name the connection and say why it matters. In Lesson 
Two, Scenario B changes the fault: the AP stays powered, but the cable from the AP to the switch is 
unplugged. The laptop can still be associated with Wi-Fi, yet it cannot reach the wired server through 
that route. Comparing this with AP power off helps students separate wireless association from 
onward delivery. If the gap is about TCP/IP instead, we use Follow-up B and check a fresh missing-
part case. Students who can already explain the core ideas can try the separate application task. E 
will finish by explaining why we chose the tool and how we would evaluate the lesson.
