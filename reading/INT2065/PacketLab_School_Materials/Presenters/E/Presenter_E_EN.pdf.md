Presenter E: Educational value and 
responsible use
25-minute presentation   /   Slides 38–39   /   20:00–23:00
Slide 38. What the model adds   20:00–21:25
Stage cue (do not read): Link educational value to the controlled test. Explain one limitation of the model.
What does the model add to a printed diagram? A diagram is useful for pointing to the route. Packet 
Lab lets us send along the same route, change one connection, and compare the two outcomes. 
That repeatable comparison makes the student's explanation more concrete. They can point to what 
changed and explain why one transfer failed while another path might still work. The simulation is 
deliberately simplified. The numbered parts are teaching labels, the Internet cloud summarises 
onward networks, and the provider uplink is not a modem model. We explain those limits when we 
teach, so students know which ideas the model represents and which details are outside it. I would 
keep the route diagram visible during the first explanation and use the simulation only when we are 
ready to change a condition. On the next test, students take over the controls. That gives them a 
chance to check their own prediction against the evidence.
Slide 39. Access, privacy and AI use   21:25–23:00
Stage cue (do not read): Describe the safeguards you would apply in class. Mention the offline alternative and 
teacher review of AI-assisted materials.
For classroom access, we would use fictional data and avoid asking for student logins or personal 
traffic. We prepare the site before class and check that the labels are readable from the back of the 
room. In pairs, both students get a turn, and both write an explanation. If the Internet connection 
fails, we can use the offline lab, prepared stills or paper examples. The task stays the same: predict, 
observe or inspect the given result, then explain. We would disclose any AI assistance used in 
preparing the teaching materials and check the examples and marking ourselves. The teacher keeps 
responsibility for what is taught and how answers are judged. If a student has difficulty reading the 
screen or operating the mouse, I would offer the same route in print and ask for the same 
explanation. If writing masks their understanding, the teacher can ask for an individual oral 
explanation and record how it was collected. We are assessing the network reasoning.

Presenter E: Evaluation and conclusion
25-minute presentation   /   Slides 40–41   /   23:00–25:00
Slide 40. A trial would test the design   23:00–24:35
Stage cue (do not read): Explain what you would collect in a trial and how it would affect the next lesson. Do not 
claim results.
If we trialled the lessons, we would look at three things. First, can a student explain a new route or 
missing-part case unaided? Second, can the class complete the essential tasks in the time 
available? Third, can students use and read the resources, including the paper version? For 
example, if most students predict correctly but cannot explain why the wired path still works, we 
would spend more time contrasting the two routes. If a technical issue slows the class down, we 
would move to the prepared alternative and carry any unfinished reasoning forward. I would also 
keep the student's first answer, the correction after feedback, and the answer to the fresh case 
separate. If the fresh explanation still uses the same wrong idea, the teacher revisits that concept 
even if the worksheet total looks fine. If it is secure, the student moves on to the application task. 
This is a proposed teaching sequence. We have not trialled it with a class or measured learning 
gains.
Slide 41. What should students be able to explain?   24:35–25:00
Stage cue (do not read): Close at 25:00. Then follow the lecturer’s arrangements for questions.
To finish, we want students to be able to say which route worked, what changed, and why. We give 
them references to get started, use Packet Lab to test their ideas, and then collect an explanation 
they can give on their own. Thank you.
