## 第 3 页

1. Introduction
ICT Learning Companion is a mobile app for Hong Kong secondary students (S4 to S6) who are preparing
for the ICT subject in the HKDSE exam. Many students revise by doing a lot of past-paper questions. This
helps them get used to the exam, but it does not tell them why an answer is wrong, and it does not point
them to the topics they are weakest in. My app is designed for these two problems.
From the start, I wanted the AI in this app to find out what the student does not know yet and help them
work out the answer by themselves. The app has four AI features for this. A short pre-test with ten
questions checks what a new student already knows. The results go onto a knowledge map, which marks
each point as mastered, needing review, or not tested enough. The app then uses this map to make a daily
study plan that fits the exam date and the time the student has each day. When the student gets a question
wrong, the mistake guide asks one small question for each step of the solution, and the solution itself only
appears if the student asks for it. The step where the student gets stuck is saved to the map, so the next
day’s plan changes.
A new user signs up, does the pre-test and sees the result, while a returning user just logs in. Both then go
to the Today screen, where they can follow the AI plan or look through the Learn section before they start
practising. A wrong answer opens the mistake guide. At the end of the guide, the AI gives a short recap and
the student goes back to practice. The tab bar at the bottom also leads to Ask AI, Insights and the Me
screen. There are twelve screens in total, and I used the same numbers (1 to 12) in the wireframes, the UI
and the storyboard so that they are easy to compare.
2. Evaluation of Existing Applications
I chose three apps that show different ways students already study on their phones. Khan Academy is a
course platform with an AI tutor, Photomath is an AI problem solver, and Quizlet is a flashcard and
practice app. Each has more than 100,000 ratings on the App Store. Between them they cover lessons,
practice and AI help with mistakes, which my app tries to bring together. I compared them on five criteria
based on what an HKDSE ICT student needs (Table 1). The ratings are App Store (US) and Google Play
numbers from an app data website, checked on 11 November 2026, and prices are in US dollars.
2.1 Khan Academy
Khan Academy is a free, non-profit platform with video lessons and practice in maths, science, computing
and other subjects (Khan Academy, n.d.). It has a rating of 4.6 out of 5 on the App Store (about 104,000
ratings) and 4.2 out of 5 on Google Play (about 172,000 ratings) (Marlvel, n.d.-a). Its AI tutor, Khanmigo,
costs US$4 per month or US$44 per year and guides students with questions (Kids AI Tools, n.d.).

Khan Academy has a large library of good free content, and it tracks the student’s mastery of each skill.
Khanmigo is the closest to what I wanted to build, because it asks students to explain their thinking. The

they cannot use it (Khan Academy Help Center, n.d.). The content also follows the US curriculum and does
not cover the HKDSE ICT syllabus, and some functions on the website are missing from the mobile app.
2.2 Photomath
Photomath is made by Google. Students take a photo of a maths question and the app shows the answer
with step-by-step methods (Google, n.d.). It has a rating of 4.8 out of 5 on the App Store (about 733,000
ratings) and 4.3 out of 5 on Google Play (about 3.1 million ratings) (Marlvel, n.d.-b). The basic version is
free, and the detailed explanations need a paid Photomath Plus plan.

Photomath is fast. One scan gives a full worked solution, often with more than one method, which is
helpful for checking homework. The same speed causes a problem for learning: the whole solution appears
at once, so students can copy it without thinking through the steps. The full explanation of each step is only
in the paid plan, which one reviewer complains about (Apptail, n.d.). Photomath also covers maths only,
and it does not keep track of which topics a student is weak in.
2.3 Quizlet
Quizlet is a flashcard app. Students make their own study sets or search for sets made by others, and study
them in Flashcards, Learn, Test and Match modes (Quizlet Inc., n.d.). It has a rating of 4.8 out of 5 on the
App Store (about 1.1 million ratings) and 4.7 out of 5 on Google Play (about 885,000 ratings) (Marlvel,
n.d.-c). The free version has ads, and Quizlet Plus costs US$7.99 per month or US$35.99 per year (Nibble,
n.d.).

Quizlet has study sets for almost every subject. Its Learn mode keeps showing the terms a student gets
wrong until they remember them, and short study sessions on a phone are easy. The sets are made by users,
though, so their quality varies and they are not based on the HKDSE ICT syllabus. When a student answers
wrongly, Quizlet shows the correct answer but cannot tell which step of their thinking went wrong. Its AI
tutor, Q-Chat, was removed in June 2025 (AI Tools Atlas, n.d.). Free users can now do only a limited
number of Learn and Test rounds, and many users have left bad reviews about this change (Nibble, n.d.).
2.4 Comparative Summary
I gave each app a score from 1 (not supported) to 5 (fully supported) for each criterion (Table 1). None of
the three apps fully supports the HKDSE syllabus, mistake correction and exam planning at the same time.

3. Competitive Advantages
No app meets all five criteria for an HKDSE ICT student. Khanmigo already guides students with
questions, so I do not count guided help alone as new. What my app adds is guided help, gap analysis and
exam planning built around one syllabus and open to Hong Kong students. This gives it three advantages
over the apps above.
First, revision starts from what each student does not know. Quizlet and Photomath do not follow the
HKDSE ICT syllabus, and none of the three apps plans revision around an exam date (criteria C1 and C4).
In my app, the pre-test creates the knowledge map, and the daily plan puts the weak points first while
fitting the exam date and the student’s free time each day (screens 2, 3 and 4). Using information about
what students do not know yet to decide what they study next is one of the most effective ways to improve
results (Black & Wiliam, 1998).
Second, the student fixes their own mistakes. Take a student who adds two 8-bit binary numbers, gets 150
and does not notice the overflow. Photomath would show the full working, Quizlet would show the correct
answer, and Khanmigo cannot be used in Hong Kong (criterion C3). My mistake guide asks three short
questions: What is the sum in decimal? What range can 8-bit two’s complement hold? What does this mean
for the result? The student checks each step, gets a hint when they are wrong, and sees the answer only if
they ask for it (screens 7, 8 and 9). Explaining an answer and trying to recall it yourself leads to better
understanding and memory than reading the solution (Chi et al., 1994; Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
Third, fixing a mistake changes what the student studies next, which none of the other three apps does. The
guide saves the step where the student got stuck, the knowledge map is updated, and the plan brings that
point back on a later day (screens 9, 11 and 4). Practice works best when it focuses on specific weak points
and is repeated over time (Ericsson et al., 1993), and the app does this scheduling for the student.

4. Wireframes
The wireframes (Appendix A, Figures A1 to A5) are low fidelity and in greyscale, so the focus stays on
layout and interaction. They follow the user flow in Section 1 and are split into the same four parts as the
storyboard: onboarding (screens 1 to 3), daily learning (screens 4 to 6), the AI mistake guide (screens 7 to
9) and other app features (screens 10 to 12). Figure A1 shows how all twelve screens are connected.
Every element that can be tapped has a number next to it. The note under each screen gives the name of the
element and the screen it leads to, using the same button text as the UI, for example “Guide me step by step
→ 8”. Arrows between screens name the action that takes the user to the next screen. My first versions had
more screens with more text on each. I merged screens that were very similar and removed a separate
welcome section, which brought the app down to twelve screens. To keep cognitive load low, each screen
has one main button near the bottom and at most one other action. Back links are always at the top left, and
the five-tab bar stays in the same place. Everything the AI produces sits in a box with a thick border and an
“AI” label, so students can tell which content comes from the AI.
5. User Interface Design
Revision can be stressful, so I wanted the UI (Appendix B, Figures B1 to B3) to feel calm. It uses a warm
off-white background, white cards with round corners and plenty of space, and each screen shows only
what the student needs for the next step. I also tried a more artistic style, but it pulled attention away from
the content, so I went back to the simpler one. Each colour has one meaning (Table 2). Purple marks
everything the AI produces; I chose it because it looks very different from the green used for learning
actions, so the two are never confused. Amber marks points that need review, and red appears only on
wrong answers.

6. Storyboard with Input and Output
The storyboard (Appendix C, Figures C1 and C2) is a flowchart split into the same four parts as the
wireframes. Circles show where the flow starts and ends, and diamonds show decisions with YES and NO
paths. Each screen card has a small screenshot of the UI and its screen number. Grey tags show the input
from the student, and teal tags show the output from the app.
The decision “Have an account?” sends returning users to log in and new users to sign up and do the pre-
test. On the Today screen, “Follow AI plan?” either goes straight to practice or goes through the Learn
section and a knowledge point first. In practice, “Answer correct?” leads to the next question, and a wrong
answer opens the mistake guide. In the guide, “Step right?” gives a hint and another try, and “All 3 steps
done?” leads to the AI recap and back to practice. The tab bar leads to Ask AI, Insights and the Me screen,
and the flow ends with “Log out?”. Table 3 gives the input and output of each screen.

8. Limitations and Future Work
This project is a design prototype. The AI analysis, study plan and mistake guide are designed but not yet
connected to a real AI service, and the questions and numbers on the screens are examples. I think the
biggest weakness of my design is how much depends on the first ten pre-test questions. If the knowledge
map starts with a wrong picture of the student, the first few plans will be less useful until there is enough
practice data. AI can also make mistakes, so every AI card lets the student report a problem, and the full
answer is always available on request. I have not tested the design with real students yet. My next steps
would be a small usability test with S5 students, a real AI service on a server, a Traditional Chinese version
and a teacher view showing the weak points of a whole class.
