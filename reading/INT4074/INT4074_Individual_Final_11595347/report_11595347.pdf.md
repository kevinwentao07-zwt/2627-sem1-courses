## 第 2 页

ICT Learning Companion: Design of an AI-Supported Revision App for HKDSE 
Information and Communication Technology
ICT Learning Companion is a mobile app for Hong Kong secondary students (S4 to S6) 
who are preparing for the ICT subject in the HKDSE exam. Many students revise by doing a lot 
of past-paper questions. This helps them get used to the exam, but it does not tell them why an 
answer is wrong, and it does not point them to the topics they are weakest in. My app is designed 
for these two problems.
From the start, I wanted the AI in this app to find out what the student does not know yet 
and help them work out the answer by themselves. The app has four AI features for this. A short 
pre-test with ten questions checks what a new student already knows. The results go onto a 
knowledge map, which marks each point as mastered, needing review, or not tested enough. The 
app then uses this map to make a daily study plan that fits the exam date and the time the student 
has each day. When the student gets a question wrong, the mistake guide asks one small question 
for each step of the solution, and the solution itself only appears if the student asks for it. The 
step where the student gets stuck is saved to the map, so the next day’s plan changes.
A new user signs up, does the pre-test and sees the result, while a returning user just logs 
in. Both then go to the Today screen, where they can follow the AI plan or look through the 
Learn section before they start practising. A wrong answer opens the mistake guide. At the end 
of the guide, the AI gives a short recap and the student goes back to practice. The tab bar at the 
bottom also leads to Ask AI, Insights and the Me screen. There are twelve screens in total, and I 
used the same numbers (1 to 12) in the wireframes, the UI and the storyboard so that they are 
easy to compare.

Evaluation of Existing Applications
I chose three apps that show different ways students already study on their phones. Khan 
Academy is a course platform with an AI tutor, Photomath is an AI problem solver, and Quizlet 
is a flashcard and practice app. Each has more than 100,000 ratings on the App Store. Between 
them they cover lessons, practice and AI help with mistakes, which my app tries to bring 
together. I compared them on five criteria based on what an HKDSE ICT student needs (Table 
1). The ratings are App Store (US) and Google Play numbers from an app data website, checked 
on 11 November 2026, and prices are in US dollars.
Khan Academy
Khan Academy is a free, non-profit platform with video lessons and practice in maths, 
science, computing and other subjects (Khan Academy, n.d.). Figure 1 shows its mastery and 
practice screens. It has a rating of 4.6 out of 5 on the App Store (about 104,000 ratings) and 4.2 
out of 5 on Google Play (about 172,000 ratings) (Marlvel, n.d.-a). Its AI tutor, Khanmigo, costs 
US$4 per month or US$44 per year and guides students with questions (KidsAiTools Editorial 
Team, 2026).

Competitive Advantages
No app meets all five criteria for an HKDSE ICT student. Khanmigo already guides 
students with questions, so I do not count guided help alone as new. What my app adds is guided

## 第 8 页

8
help, gap analysis and exam planning built around one syllabus and open to Hong Kong students. 
This gives it three advantages over the apps above.
First, revision starts from what each student does not know. Quizlet and Photomath do 
not follow the HKDSE ICT syllabus, and none of the three apps plans revision around an exam 
date (criteria C1 and C4). In my app, the pre-test creates the knowledge map, and the daily plan 
puts the weak points first while fitting the exam date and the student’s free time each day 
(screens 2, 3 and 4). Using information about what students do not know yet to decide what they 
study next is one of the most effective ways to improve results (Black & Wiliam, 1998).
Second, the student fixes their own mistakes. Take a student who adds two 8-bit binary 
numbers, gets 150 and does not notice the overflow. Photomath would show the full working, 
Quizlet would show the correct answer, and Khanmigo cannot be used in Hong Kong (criterion 
C3). My mistake guide asks three short questions: What is the sum in decimal? What range can 
8-bit two’s complement hold? What does this mean for the result? The student checks each step, 
gets a hint when they are wrong, and sees the answer only if they ask for it (screens 7, 8 and 9). 
Explaining an answer and trying to recall it yourself leads to better understanding and memory 
than reading the solution (Chi et al., 1994; Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).
Third, fixing a mistake changes what the student studies next, which none of the other 
three apps does. The guide saves the step where the student got stuck, the knowledge map is 
updated, and the plan brings that point back on a later day (screens 9, 11 and 4). Practice works 
best when it focuses on specific weak points and is repeated over time (Ericsson et al., 1993), 
and the app does this scheduling for the student.

Wireframes
The wireframes (Appendix A, Figures A1 to A5) are low fidelity and in greyscale, so the 
focus stays on layout and interaction. They follow the user flow described at the start of the 
report and are split into the same four parts as the storyboard: onboarding (screens 1 to 3), daily 
learning (screens 4 to 6), the AI mistake guide (screens 7 to 9) and other app features (screens 10 
to 12). Figure A1 shows how all twelve screens are connected.
Every element that can be tapped has a number next to it. The note under each screen 
gives the name of the element and the screen it leads to, using the same button text as the UI, for 
example “Guide me step by step → 8”. Arrows between screens name the action that takes the 
user to the next screen. My first versions had more screens with more text on each. I merged 
screens that were very similar and removed a separate welcome section, which brought the app 
down to twelve screens. To keep cognitive load low, each screen has one main button near the 
bottom and at most one other action. Back links are always at the top left, and the five-tab bar 
stays in the same place. Everything the AI produces sits in a box with a thick border and an “AI” 
label, so students can tell which content comes from the AI.
User Interface Design
Revision can be stressful, so I wanted the UI (Appendix B, Figures B1 to B3) to feel 
calm. It uses a warm off-white background, white cards with round corners and plenty of space, 
and each screen shows only what the student needs for the next step. I also tried a more artistic 
style, but it pulled attention away from the content, so I went back to the simpler one. Each 
colour has one meaning (Table 2). Purple marks everything the AI produces; I chose it because it 
looks very different from the green used for learning actions, so the two are never confused. 
Amber marks points that need review, and red appears only on wrong answers.

Limitations and Future Work
This project is a design prototype. The AI analysis, study plan and mistake guide are 
designed but not yet connected to a real AI service, and the questions and numbers on the screens 
are examples. I think the biggest weakness of my design is how much depends on the first ten 
pre-test questions. If the knowledge map starts with a wrong picture of the student, the first few 
plans will be less useful until there is enough practice data. AI can also make mistakes, so every 
AI card lets the student report a problem, and the full answer is always available on request. I 
have not tested the design with real students yet. My next steps would be a small usability test 
with S5 students, a real AI service on a server, a Traditional Chinese version and a teacher view 
showing the weak points of a whole class.
