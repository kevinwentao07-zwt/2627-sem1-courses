## 第 2 页

Part 1　Philosophy of Science
Q1　Falsification 
原题 Q1 [9 marks]
(a) Define "falsifiability" in Popper's sense. [2]
(b) Explain why the statement "All swans are white" can be falsified but can never be verified. [3]
(c) A newspaper headline says: "Science has proven that vaccines work." Evaluate this wording from
Popper's perspective. [4]
(a) A theory is falsifiable if it makes predictions that could in principle be shown false by observation or
experiment. It does not mean the theory is false.
(b) Verification would require checking every swan, past, present and future, which is impossible because
all possible tests can never be performed (L1 p.10). A single black swan logically refutes the universal
statement → asymmetry between verification and falsification.
(c) Strictly, science cannot "prove" a theory true; a theory that survives repeated, risky tests is
corroborated and accepted tentatively. However, the headline captures something real: the evidence is
strong and repeated, so the claim is highly reliable. A better wording: "Strong, repeated evidence shows
vaccines work."
得分关键：(a) 一定要写出 "could in principle"；(c) 正反两面都要有，最后给出更准确的写法。
常见失分：(c) 只写"Popper 说不能证明"，没有承认证据其实很强。
Q2　Science, pseudoscience and non-science 
原题 Q2 [8 marks]
(a) Classify each claim as science, pseudoscience or non-science, and justify each answer. [6]
(i) "Drug X lowers blood pressure by at least 10 mmHg compared with a placebo."
(ii) "Your birth chart shows you will face challenges this year."
(iii) A poem describing the beauty of the Moon.
(b) A classmate says: "Non-science is worthless because it cannot be tested." Evaluate this claim. [2]
(i) Science: specific, measurable and falsifiable; it can be tested in a placebo-controlled trial.
(ii) Pseudoscience: presented as if based on a system (planets, birth time), but so vague that any outcome
fits → unfalsifiable; claims scientific status without meeting its standards.
(iii) Non-science: it does not claim to be science and is not meant to be tested.
(b) Disagree. Non-science (art, literature, ethics) is valuable in other ways; it is simply not judged by
experiment. Science itself "cannot provide complete answers to all questions" (L2 p.6).
得分关键：伪科学和非科学的分别在于它有没有冒充科学。

## 第 3 页

Q3　Trusting science without proof [10 marks] 
名校式
参考：LSE PH201「Explain Popper's anti-inductivist
view」；剑桥 HPS「Solve the problem of induction」
Popper says no number of observations can prove a scientific theory true. Does this mean we should
not trust science? Discuss.
1. Define　Induction = drawing a general conclusion from many observations. Popper: observations can
never prove a universal theory, but one counter-example can refute it.
2. Explain　All possible tests can never be performed (L1 p.10), so even 1,000 white swans cannot guarantee
the next one is white.
3. Apply　The green tea RCT can support the hypothesis, but a later, larger study could still overturn it; the
atom model was revised many times.
4. Objection　If nothing is ever proven, scientific claims are no better than opinions.
5. Reply　Trust can be based on how severely a theory has been tested, not on proof. A theory that survives
repeated, risky tests and peer review is far more reliable than an untested claim, and knowledge is durable
(L2 p.6).
6. Conclusion　We should trust science tentatively and in proportion to the evidence: not as certain truth,
but as the best-tested knowledge available.
得分关键：要分开 proof（证明）和 trust（信任）；反驳一定要写出来再回应。
Q4　Is falsifiability enough? [10 marks] 
名校式
参考：IB Philosophy 答题指引「定义三个类别 → 解释 Popper → 评价
局限」；LSE「How does Popper distinguish science from pseudoscience?」
Is falsifiability alone sufficient to distinguish science from pseudoscience? Use examples from the
course.
1. Define　Falsifiability: a claim must make predictions that could be shown false. Pseudoscience claims
scientific status without meeting scientific standards.
2. Explain　Falsifiability rules out claims that fit every outcome, e.g. vague horoscopes.
3. Apply　"Drug X lowers blood pressure by ≥10 mmHg vs placebo" is falsifiable → science; "you will face
challenges this year" is not → pseudoscience.
4. Objection　Some pseudoscientific claims are falsifiable, e.g. "this engine gives 150 W for 100 W input" can
be tested and shown false. Yet the sellers keep making the claim after it fails.
5. Reply　So what matters is also how people respond to tests. The L3 table adds willingness to change with
new evidence, peer review, repeatable results, inviting criticism and limited claims of usefulness.
6. Conclusion　Falsifiability is a necessary first test but not sufficient on its own; it works best with the other
features in the science vs pseudoscience table.
这是最典型的名校题：先肯定 Popper，再指出他的局限，最后用课程里的其他标准补足。

## 第 4 页

Q5　Saving a theory from refutation [10 marks] 
名校式
参考：LSE PH201 讨论题「一个能被调整成符合任何数据的
理论，有什么问题？」；Twente 科学哲学期末考
When a prediction fails, a believer can always add an extra explanation to save the theory. Compare the
apple example with the psychic who blames sceptics' "negative energy". When is adding an explanation
acceptable, and when is it unscientific?
1. Define　A failed prediction normally counts against a hypothesis (the "No" arrow in the flowchart).
2. Explain　But a prediction may fail for other reasons, e.g. the measuring instrument is not sensitive
enough.
3. Apply　Apple example: "no attraction between an apple and an orange" led to a new, testable question
(does attraction depend on mass?), which can be checked with larger masses or better instruments.
Psychic: "negative energy" cannot be measured or checked; it is added only to protect the claim.
4. Objection　If any extra explanation is allowed, nothing can ever be falsified.
5. Reply　The difference is whether the added explanation can itself be tested and could fail. Untestable
excuses match the pseudoscience features (fixed ideas, criticism seen as hostile).
6. Conclusion　Adding explanations is acceptable when it leads to new testable predictions; it is unscientific
when it only shields the theory from evidence.
不用写 Duhem–Quine 这个名词（超纲），用苹果例子和 L3 的伪科学特征说出同样的道理就够了。
Q6　Responding to failed predictions 
原题 Q3 [9 marks]
A psychic's predictions fail in a controlled test. She replies: "My powers do not work when sceptics are
present, because their negative energy blocks me."
(a) Identify TWO features of pseudoscience from the science vs pseudoscience comparison that her
response shows. [2]
(b) Using Popper's ideas, explain why her response makes her claim less scientific. [4]
(c) Explain how a scientist should respond to a failed prediction, with reference to the scientific method
flowchart. [3]
(a) Fixed ideas (no willingness to change with new evidence); sees criticism as hostile/conspiracy; non-
repeatable results.
(b) Each failure is explained away by an added excuse that cannot itself be tested. Whatever happens
(success → power works; failure → sceptics blocked it), the claim survives. A claim compatible with every
possible outcome cannot be falsified, so it is not scientific.
(c) Accept the result, then revise or replace the hypothesis ("No" arrow), test again and report openly so
others can check (peer review). The scientist does not protect the hypothesis at all costs.

## 第 5 页

Q7　Is science always correct? 
原题 Q5 [9 marks]
(a) Outline how the model of the atom changed from Democritus to quarks. [3]
(b) Explain what this history shows about the statement "Is science always correct? No…" (L1 p.10). [3]
(c) Explain how scientific knowledge can be both "subject to change" and "durable", using this
example. [3]
(a) Democritus: indivisible particles → Thomson: electrons → Rutherford: nucleus → Ivanenko: protons
and neutrons → SLAC 1968: quarks.
(b) Each model was accepted, then revised when new evidence appeared. Since all tests can never be
performed, any theory may be overturned; science advances through continuous falsification and
improvement; good scientists are sceptical of their own findings.
(c) Change: the model was repeatedly revised. Durable: each new model kept what earlier ones got right
(e.g. matter is made of particles; electrons still exist) and added detail. Change is mostly refinement, not
total rejection.
Q8　If past theories were wrong… [10 marks] 
名校式
参考：LSE PH201 短答题「Pessimistic Meta-Induction」的
问法（只借问法，不用这个名词）
"The model of the atom has been replaced many times. So today's scientific theories will probably turn
out wrong too, and we should not believe them." Discuss.
1. Define　Scientific ideas are subject to change, but scientific knowledge is durable (L2 p.6).
2. Explain　History shows revision: Democritus → Thomson → Rutherford → protons and neutrons →
quarks.
3. Apply　Each model kept what earlier ones got right (matter is made of particles; electrons still exist) and
added more accurate detail; Newton's laws still work in everyday situations; Pluto's observed data
remained valid after reclassification.
4. Objection　The argument has some force: future evidence may indeed revise current theories, so
certainty is not justified (Popper).
5. Reply　But "will be revised" ≠ "is simply wrong". Change is mostly refinement, so current theories are
the best-tested so far and likely to keep their correct core.
6. Conclusion　We should hold current theories tentatively, not reject them; the history of revision shows
science self-correcting, which is a reason for trust, not distrust.
高分关键：区分'被修正'和'完全错误'。

## 第 6 页

Q9　What is right — and incomplete — in Popper? [10 marks] 
名校式
参考：剑桥 HPS Part IB 2001「What is
right in Karl Popper's philosophy of science?」
What is right, and what is incomplete, in Popper's view of science? Refer to the course.
1. Define　Popper: scientific theories must be falsifiable; they are tested, never proven, and accepted only
tentatively.
2. Explain　Right: it explains why science avoids certainty, why the flowchart loops back to testing, and why
vague or excuse-protected claims (astrology) are unscientific.
3. Apply　Atom models were revised through falsification; good scientists are sceptical of their own findings
(L1 p.10).
4. Objection　Incomplete: (i) scientists do not drop a hypothesis at the first negative result (apple example:
they ask a new question); (ii) science also builds durable knowledge, not only refutations; (iii) Popper
focuses on logic, but the scientific enterprise of peer review, replication and burden of proof also decides
what is accepted (L2 p.13–15).
5. Reply　These points add to Popper rather than overturn him: falsification remains central, but it works
within a community and over time.
6. Conclusion　Popper is right about the core logic of testing, but a full picture of science needs the
scientific enterprise and the durability of knowledge.
要写出'对的部分'和'不完整的部分'两边，每一边都要有课程里的例子。
Q10　Occam's Razor 
原题 Q9 [10 marks]
(a) State Occam's Razor and explain what "simpler" means. [3]
(b) Apply it to the two scenarios for a "fuel-less engine" (A: it does not work and is sold for profit; B: it
really creates energy). [3]
(c) Explain TWO precautions in using Occam's Razor, using the "pyramids were too difficult, so aliens
built them" claim. [4]
(a) When two theories make the same predictions, the simpler one is better. "Simpler" means requiring
fewer extra, unsupported assumptions, not fewer words or being easier to understand.
(b) A needs one common assumption (the seller wants profit). B needs many: energy conservation is
wrong, all scientists missed it, power companies ignore it, and so on. → Prefer A.
(c) ① It is not proof: the simpler explanation is not always correct, so evidence must decide. ② It cannot
justify supernatural explanations: "aliens" sounds short but needs many unsupported assumptions, while
the human explanation is supported by evidence (workers' villages, tools). Also, "too difficult" only shows
a gap in our knowledge, not evidence for aliens.

## 第 7 页

Part 2　Scientific Method
Q11　The flowchart 
原题 Q11 [9 marks]
(a) Describe the steps of the scientific method flowchart. [3]
(b) Explain why the "Yes" arrow returns to "Test hypothesis" rather than ending the process. [3]
(c) Explain the purpose of a pilot study and what it can NOT show. [3]
(a) Identify the problem → gather data (research) → hypothesis → test (experiment) → do the data agree?
No → revise/replace the hypothesis; Yes → test again.
(b) Agreement does not prove the hypothesis; all possible tests can never be done, and future evidence
may refute it → continuous testing (Popper).
(c) A small-scale preliminary study to check whether the methods, measures and procedures are feasible
before the main study. It cannot show that the hypothesis is true, because the sample is small and it is not
designed as a full test.
Q12　The apple example 
原题 Q12 [8 marks]
(a) Explain the four steps of the apple example (question, hypothesis, prediction & tests, analysis). [4]
(b) Test (ii) "an apple attracts an orange" gave a negative result. Explain why this does not end the
investigation, and propose ONE next step. [4]
(a) Q: why do apples fall? H: any two objects with mass attract each other. Predictions: (i) Earth attracts an
orange; (ii) an apple attracts an orange. Analysis: (i) positive; (ii) apparently no attraction.
(b) A negative result raises a new question (is the force proportional to mass?), so science is cyclical. The
attraction may be too small to detect, so the result may reflect the limits of measurement. Next step: test
whether attraction increases with mass (use much larger masses) or use a more sensitive instrument.
Q13　Data first or hypothesis first? [10 marks] 
名校式
参考：剑桥 HPS Part IB 2009 Q1（Sherlock Holmes：'It is a
capital mistake to theorise before one has data'）
"It is a capital mistake to theorise before one has data. One begins to twist facts to suit theories." Is this
good advice for scientists? Discuss with reference to the flowchart and the apple example.
1. Define　The flowchart places "gather data (research)" before "hypothesis".
2. Explain　Supports the quote: theorising too early risks bias, i.e. selecting only favourable data (cherry
picking, L3 p.17), which pseudoscience does.
3. Apply　Against the quote: in the apple example, the question and hypothesis came first and decided
what to test (Earth–orange, apple–orange). Without a hypothesis, we would not know which data to
collect. Popper also sees science as bold hypotheses followed by tests.
4. Objection　If hypotheses come first, won't scientists twist the data?
5. Reply　Bias is controlled by method, not by avoiding hypotheses: randomisation, control groups,
blinding, peer review and willingness to revise.
6. Conclusion　Partly good advice: do not bend facts to fit a theory. But science needs hypotheses to guide
observation; the real safeguard is testing them fairly and accepting the result.
名校论述题一定要有两面，最后的结论要有限定（'部分同意'）。

## 第 8 页

Q14　Is the scientific method a recipe? [8 marks] 
名校式
参考：剑桥 HPS「Is philosophy of science about what
science is, or what it ought to be?」的问法
"The scientific method is a fixed step-by-step recipe." Evaluate this statement.
1. Define　The flowchart shows steps: problem → data → hypothesis → test → analysis.
2. Explain　These steps make science systematic and repeatable.
3. Apply　But the flowchart has loops (data agree → test again; disagree → revise); a pilot study may change
the design; a negative result can open a new question (apple example).
4. Objection　Steps on paper look like a recipe.
5. Reply　Forming hypotheses needs imagination as well as logic (L2 p.11–12), and results feed back into
new questions, so it is a cycle, not a one-way list.
6. Conclusion　The method has a clear structure but is iterative and creative, so it is better described as a
cycle of testing than a fixed recipe.
Q15　Observational data vs experiment 
原题 Q13 [10 marks]
Data from 40,000 adults show that people who drink more green tea have lower CVD rates (40, 36, 31, 30
cases per 1,000 for 0, 1–2, 3–4, 5+ cups a day). Heavier tea drinkers also exercised more.
(a) Explain why these data do not show that tea causes lower CVD risk. [3]
(b) Design a study following the green tea example on L1 p.9. [5]
(c) State what result would count against the hypothesis. [2]
(a) People chose how much to drink, so the groups may differ in other ways (exercise) that also affect CVD;
association ≠ causation.
(b) Hypothesis (with mechanism); random assignment to daily tea or a control group (e.g. a similar-
looking drink without tea); same duration and measurement for both; compare CVD incidence; test for
statistical significance; conclusion; if supported, study mechanisms and dosage.
(c) No statistically significant difference between groups, or a higher rate in the tea group.
Q16　Designing a fair test 
原题 Q14 [10 marks]
A company claims its cooling paint lowers roof temperature (L6). Design an experiment to test this
claim. Include: hypothesis, independent and dependent variables, control, controlled variables,
procedure, analysis and how you would reach a conclusion.
H: roofs with the paint have a lower surface temperature than identical roofs with ordinary paint.
IV: type of paint; DV: roof surface temperature (and air-conditioning energy use).
Control: an identical roof section with ordinary paint, ideally half of the same roof.
Controlled variables: weather, time of measurement, roof material, orientation, measuring instrument.
Procedure: pilot study → measure both halves at the same times over several weeks; repeat on several
buildings; use independent testers to avoid bias.
Analysis: compare means; is the difference statistically significant? Conclusion: support or reject; report
the limitations.
常见失分：只拿今年夏天和去年夏天比较。天气不同，变量没有控制。

Q17　Interpreting trial data 
原题 Q15 [9 marks]

Improved
A (patients know what they get)

(b) What does Trial B suggest about how the bracelet "works"? [3]

Q18　Threats to a fair comparison 
原题 Q16 [9 marks]
Students are randomly assigned to face-to-face, online or hybrid teaching (100 each). Before the final
test, 5, 40 and 6 students drop out respectively. Mean scores of those tested: 72, 68, 75.
(a) Identify the independent and dependent variables. [2]
(b) Explain how the dropout pattern threatens the conclusion. [4]
(c) Suggest TWO improvements. [3]
(a) IV: teaching mode; DV: test score.
(b) Random assignment made the groups comparable at the start, but the much higher online dropout
means those tested may no longer be comparable. If weaker students dropped out, the online mean (68)
is overestimated, so the comparison is biased.
(c) Track and report all dropouts and their reasons; analyse the results with and without dropouts; reduce
dropout (support, reminders); use the same teacher for all modes; keep content and test identical.

## 第 10 页

Q19　Personal experience as evidence [8 marks] 
名校式
参考：剑桥 HPS Part IB 2005「Which is more likely to be
true: science or common sense?」
"I tried crystal healing and my headache went away, so it works." Explain why personal experience is
weaker evidence than a controlled trial. Is personal experience ever useful in science?
1. Define　A controlled trial compares a treatment group with a control or placebo group under the same
conditions.
2. Explain　A single experience cannot separate the treatment from the placebo effect, natural recovery and
chance; people also remember successes more than failures (selecting favourable cases).
3. Apply　Crystal trials: when patients knew what they used, 70% vs 30% improved; double-blind, 52% vs
50% (not significant), so the "effect" came mainly from expectation.
4. Objection　Personal experience still feels convincing and is sometimes right.
5. Reply　It can be useful as an observation that raises a question or hypothesis (the first steps of the
flowchart), but it must then be tested.
6. Conclusion　Personal experience can start an investigation but cannot settle it; only controlled,
repeatable tests can.
Q20　Short essay 
原题 Q20 [12 marks]
"Science is a method, not just a collection of facts." Discuss this statement with reference to Popper's
ideas, the scientific method and at least TWO examples from the course.
Introduction / position: Science is best understood as a self-correcting method for testing ideas,
although it does produce reliable, durable knowledge.
Method (Popper): Theories must be falsifiable and are accepted only tentatively. The flowchart loops
back to testing even after agreement. Example: atom models revised repeatedly.
Method (procedure): hypothesis → prediction → controlled test → analysis. Example: the green tea RCT
(randomisation, control, statistical significance); Mendel's 3 : 1 prediction.
Counter-point: Science also gives durable knowledge (Pluto's data remained valid; Newton still useful in
daily life), so "not just facts" is more accurate than "not facts".
Contrast with pseudoscience: astrology has "facts" and claims, but lacks the method (no falsifiable
predictions, no peer review, no willingness to change).
Conclusion: What makes knowledge scientific is how it is tested and revised, so the method is what gives
the facts their reliability.

p.48 的讨论写法：不要非黑即白，要处理反方论点。）
