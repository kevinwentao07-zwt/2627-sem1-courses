Given the fact that large language models (LLMs) can give you summaries, concepts, arguments, outlines, and even full-fledged essays, the real challenge now is not whether students need to make use of AI, but what they think of their own. The present project explores:
Research question: 
When does AI support students' thinking, and when does it begin to substitute for it?
Analytical Framework
Our analysis will proceed by evaluating AI use relative to the learning objective of the task. Core intellectual labor refers to the cognitive work that must be done by the student in order to develop or demonstrate the capacity under assessment, like comprehending evidence, creating a thesis, comparing interpretations, formulating arguments and making judgments. Therefore, "language" and "intellectual labor" cannot be considered as fixed categories. If rewriting serves merely for improving language, then it does not belong to the intellectual labor. However, if rewriting changes interpretation, evidence or argumentation, then it belongs to the intellectual labor. AI use represents cognitive outsourcing, if AI performs the key cognitive processes involved in the intended learning objective, but not just reduces the secondary processes.
Analysis will also consider AI literacy: the competence of understanding limitations of AI, critical evaluation of its output and responsible usage of it (OECD/European Commission, 2026; UNESCO, 2024). It means that lower AI involvement does not guarantee thinking, while higher AI involvement does not mean lack of thinking. What is more important are the cognitive functions performed by AI, core intellectual labor performed by the student and verification/reconstruction/final decision-making by the student.
Evidence and Approach
The research will compare three ways of involvement of AI in the writing process, namely language-oriented assistance, partial intellectual involvement, and cognitive outsourcing. Experimental research will determine whether each of them reflects the difference in learning, engagement, and agency. Feedback provided by AI can assist students in writing and revising their work when they do it themselves (Mahapatra, 2024), however, students need to evaluate and reject AI feedback (Chen et al., 2024). The more intense AI involvement can be helpful in terms of improving performance while posing problems in terms of agency, ownership, and retention of the knowledge obtained (Shi et al., 2026; Kosmyna et al., 2025).
Expected Argument and Significance
For that reason, we approach 
learning assistance, human-AI partnership, and cognitive delegation as possibilities of analysis and not as predefined categories,
 whose definitions depend on the goals of learning as well as the cognitive efforts which are kept by the learner and his or her AI literacy.
As far as global education is concerned, the question of the future of intellectual labor lies in the way that education will reshape the division of cognitive labor between humans and AI. The high degree of student utilization of AI and the Hong Kong education policy of 2026 concerning the digital education will allow us to examine this particular case from the angle of how AI literacy could go further than "how to use AI" into what to delegate and what not to delegate to AI (Education Bureau, 2026).
