Background of the lesson
Target students
Form 5 ICT students taking Elective B, Web Application Development. About 25 students, one computer each, in a computer room with a teacher display. Coding experience is mixed.
Prior knowledge.
 Students have completed Compulsory Part Module C(c), Elementary Web Authoring. They can recognise the basic constructs of HTML and discuss how web pages are organised for an intended audience, but they were not required to memorise HTML codes (Curriculum Development Council [CDC] & Hong Kong Examinations and Assessment Authority [HKEAA], 2021, pp. 24, 43). Most have not written HTML by hand, so this lesson moves them from recognising HTML to writing it.
Medium of instruction.
 English, with Cantonese support where needed. Portfolio Lab and the worksheets are available in English and Chinese; HTML terms stay in English.
Topic of teaching
Building a personal portfolio web page with HTML.
Curriculum link.
 Senior Secondary ICT Curriculum and Assessment Guide, Elective B, topic (b) Web Programming and Applications, (i) Web authoring and publishing: students should be able to edit the HTML code of web pages (CDC & HKEAA, 2021, p. 45).
Suggested number of lessons
Two connected lessons. Lesson 1: HTML structure (this plan). Lesson 2: CSS styling of the same page (overview near the end of this plan).
Suggested lesson time
70 minutes for each lesson (a double period).
Enquiry question
How can HTML help us present information about ourselves clearly and responsibly? The question is shown at the start of the lesson and students return to it in the closing minute.

Learning Teaching Strategies
Teaching Strategies
• 
Gagné's nine events of instruction
 give the lesson its sequence (Gagné et al., 1992). Event 3 (recall) is placed before Event 2 (objectives) so that the teacher introduces the objectives in light of the diagnostic result; the authors note that the events "do not invariably occur in this exact order" (p. 190).
• 
Activating prior knowledge.
 Prior knowledge helps only when it is activated, relevant to the task and congruent with the new content (Brod, 2021). The opening comparison and the diagnostic questions activate what students met in Module C(c) and in their everyday use of web pages.
• 
Scaffolding in the zone of proximal development
 (Vygotsky, 1978; Wood et al., 1976). Support is reduced step by step: teacher demonstration, HTML X-Ray, guided tasks with three levels of hints, a starter template, then an independent draft. Digital prompts support learning when they are designed well (Thomann & Deutscher, 2025), so the class rule is "check your work first, then take one hint". Partners act as more knowledgeable others when they explain elements and test each other's pages.
• 
Formative assessment.
 Evidence is used by the teacher, the learners and their peers to decide the next steps (Black & Wiliam, 2009); see the two assessment tables below.
• 
Hands-on project work with an interactive tool and peer review.
 A systematic review of HTML and CSS teaching in secondary schools identified these as promising approaches, although it found only nine eligible studies (Kharchenko & Semerikov, 2026).
• 
Positive classroom management.
 The plan follows Prevent → Support → Promote: clear expectations and tight, timed instructions prevent off-task behaviour; private, small-step support helps students who struggle; pair roles and behaviour-specific praise promote cooperation (Cheung, 2026; Hue & Li, 2008; Oliver, 2014); see the classroom management table below.
• 
Culturally responsive teaching.
 Students build the page from their own interests, background and language, so their experiences become a resource for learning (Chuang et al., 2020). All students attempt the same task with different amounts of support, and the feedback rule keeps the classroom safe for everyone, which correspond to academic development and prejudice reduction in Hernandez et al. (2013).

Positive Classroom Management
Situation
What the teacher does
Framework
Before independent work (prevention)
States the computer-room rule (only Portfolio Lab is open during Practice and Build) and teaches and displays the four-step help routine.
Splits each stage into short steps with a time limit. Instructions are given orally and stay on the screen.
Oliver (2014): be proactive; explicitly teach expectations and routines.
Cheung (2026, slides 5–7, 14): preventive classroom management; tight instructions.
Bluestein (2008): instructions are also available in written form.
During Practice and Build (monitoring)
Circulates, scans screens and notes who has not started. Checks progress at the end of each timed step and states the next step. Gives behaviour-specific praise, for example "You tested the link before asking for a hint."
Oliver (2014): acknowledge appropriate behaviour; monitor and adjust.
Scenario 1 (Prevent): a student opens YouTube or an online game during Build
1. Prevention: Build is split into timed steps shown on the screen: 42–48 own heading and introduction; 48–53 image with alt text; 53–58 working link, tested in the preview; 58–60 export.
2. At the end of each step the teacher checks progress and states the next step, so there is little empty time for students to drift away.
3. If a student still goes off task: proximity and a non-verbal cue, then a private correction that names the current step ("You are on step 2: add the alt text.").
4. If the student has already finished, give an extension task.
Cheung (2026, slides 5–7, 14): preventive classroom management; tight instructions.
Oliver (2014): be proactive; a continuum of responses.
Clear expectations and timed steps reduce off-task behaviour before it starts. Punishment only reacts after learning time is lost and does not tell the student what to do next.
Scenario 2 (Support): a student gives up on a Practice task and says "I can't do this"
1. After several failed attempts to repair the missing </h1>, the student stops working. The teacher goes to the student privately and asks which part is not working, without criticising in front of the class.
2. The teacher gives a smaller task first, for example changing only the text inside <h1>, then one hint for the missing </h1>.
3. When the student succeeds, the teacher gives specific encouragement ("Your heading now shows correctly.") and raises the difficulty step by step.
4. The teacher treats the refusal first as a sign of learning difficulty, not as a discipline problem.
Cheung (2026, slides 18, 24): private individual correction; a positive student–teacher relationship.
Cheung (2026): Dreikurs's assumed inadequacy is one possible explanation. The teacher does not judge the student's motive without knowing more.
Hue and Li (2008, p. 13): ask what skill the student is not yet showing before responding.
Private support protects the student's dignity and avoids confrontation; small achievable tasks rebuild confidence.
Scenario 3 (Promote): one partner controls the computer and does all the work during Explore or Share
1. Before Explore, the teacher and students agree on pair rules.
2. Partners take roles: Code Editor (uses the mouse and keyboard in HTML X-Ray or the code) and Reviewer (checks the preview and explains the element). They swap roles after each element until each partner has explained at least three elements.
3. The teacher gives behaviour-specific praise for cooperation: "I noticed that both of you took turns and explained each element to each other."
Hue and Li (2008); Cheung (2026, slides 8, 35–36): group-oriented classroom management.
Oliver (2014): behaviour-specific praise.
Sitting students together is not cooperative learning by itself. Clear, rotating roles give every student a real task, and specific praise reinforces cooperation.
