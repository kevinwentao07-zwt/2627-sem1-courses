TLS3092 Group Presentation Script
My First Digital Portfolio: Learning HTML Through Exploring, Practising and Creating
✂ marks an optional sentence; skip it if the rehearsal runs over 20 minutes.
Presenter 1 — Introduction, Rationale, Curriculum and Learning Objectives
Hi, everyone.
Today, our group would like to introduce our lesson design, titled 
"My First Digital Portfolio: Learning HTML Through Exploring, Practising and Creating."
Before we begin, a quick show of hands: who here has ever written a line of HTML?
Thank you. We asked the same question about our target students, and the answer shaped our design.
Nowadays, being able to present our skills, experiences and achievements clearly is an important ability.
✂ In Hong Kong, for example, JUPAS applicants can voluntarily submit a 
Student Learning Profile
, although a portfolio website itself is not a requirement.
So, we started thinking: why not teach ICT knowledge through a practical way of presenting one's work? This led us to the idea of a personal portfolio website.
Our aim is to help students move from simply using websites to understanding how to create them.
A systematic review by 
Kharchenko and Semerikov (2026)
 examined HTML and CSS teaching in secondary schools. It identified hands-on, project-based learning, interactive environments and peer review as promising approaches, although the evidence remains limited.
Our lesson is designed for 
Form 5 ICT students in Hong Kong
.
It follows the Hong Kong 
ICT Curriculum and Assessment Guide
: the Web Application Development elective, under web authoring and publishing, which covers editing HTML and applying CSS.
According to the Guide, these students have already met HTML in the compulsory part, where they recognise its basic constructs but are not required to memorise code. So our lesson takes them from recognising HTML to writing it.
We divide the unit into two lessons: 
Lesson 1
 on HTML structure, and 
Lesson 2
 on CSS styling of the same website.
For today's presentation, we will focus on the instructional design of 
Lesson 1, which lasts 70 minutes
.
By the end of this lesson, students are expected to achieve learning objectives in three domains.
First, in terms of 
knowledge
, students should be able to explain the functions of basic HTML elements, including headings, paragraphs, images and hyperlinks.
Second, in terms of 
skills
, they should be able to create a simple portfolio draft in HTML, and to check the preview and correct at least one error.
Third, in terms of 
attitudes
, students should choose personal information and images responsibly and provide constructive feedback to their classmates.
✂ The minimum expected outcome is a functional HTML draft containing a heading, an introduction, an image with appropriate alternative text and a working hyperlink.
With these objectives in mind, my groupmate will now explain the teaching strategies behind our lesson.
Presenter 2 — Effective Teaching Strategies and Instructional Design
Thank you.
Our lesson is structured around 
Gagné's Nine Events of Instruction
, together with 
Prior Knowledge Activation
, 
Scaffolding
 and 
Formative Assessment
 from TLS3092.
Event 1
 is to gain attention. The teacher shows two contrasting portfolio pages, and students discuss in pairs what makes one easier to understand.
Next comes 
Event 3
, recall of prior learning. Students answer three short diagnostic questions in the pre-test built into Portfolio Lab.
Only then does the teacher share the objectives and the portfolio checklist, which is 
Event 2
.
We swapped the two on purpose. 
Gagné et al. (1992)
 note that the events do not always occur in this exact order, and diagnosing first lets the teacher introduce the objectives in light of what students already know.
For 
Events 4 and 5
, presenting content and providing guidance, the teacher demonstrates 
<h1>
, 
<p>
, 
<img>
 and 
<a>
. Students then use our HTML X-Ray tool, worked examples and progressive hints.
For 
Events 6 and 7
, performance and feedback, students complete guided exercises and then build their own draft, with feedback from the platform, the teacher and a partner.
For 
Event 8
, assessing performance, the teacher reviews the drafts and the individual Exit Tickets.
Finally, for 
Event 9
, retention and transfer, the Exit Ticket asks students to repair a new link, and Lesson 2 opens with a recap quiz on today's HTML.
On prior knowledge, 
Brod (2021)
 explains that it supports learning only when it is activated, relevant and congruent with the new content.
Our students browse websites every day and met basic HTML in the compulsory part, although most have never written it by hand.
So the diagnostic questions ask about the purpose of a heading, how a reader reaches another page, and what to check before using a photo or personal information.
✂ They activate relevant knowledge without testing syntax, which students were never required to memorise.
Because the answers stay in each student's browser, the teacher reads screens while circulating and takes a quick show of hands, and then adjusts the explanation.
We also apply 
Scaffolding
, which is related to Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development and the work of Wood, Bruner and Ross.
Students first observe examples, then complete guided coding tasks, and finally create their own portfolio drafts with support available when needed.
Support also comes from peers: during the X-Ray activity, each student explains at least three elements to a partner.
Our objectives follow the revised 
Bloom's Taxonomy
 (
Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001
): students move from understanding elements to applying them and analysing errors.
To be honest, completing a starter template is mainly application; only the extension tasks approach creating.
✂ This helps us align learning objectives with teaching activities and assessment.
To support all of this, we developed an interactive platform called 
Portfolio Lab
, which my next groupmate will explain.
Presenter 3 — Instructional Material Design: Learn and Practice
Thank you.
I will explain the design of our main teaching material, 
Portfolio Lab
.
It contains three main modules: 
Learn, Practice and Build
.
（展示
 Portfolio Lab 
首頁及三個模組的截圖。）
Portfolio Lab is available through this QR code, and you are welcome to explore it after our presentation.
（展示
 Portfolio Lab 
的
 QR Code 
和網站網址。）
Let's begin with the 
Learn module
.
One challenge for beginners is that source code feels abstract. They can recognise a heading or an image on a webpage, but not how it relates to the underlying HTML.
To address this challenge, we developed a feature called 
HTML X-Ray
.
（展示
 HTML X-Ray 
的靜態截圖：左邊為網頁，右邊為對應
 HTML 
程式碼。
）
✂ This feature allows students to select elements on a webpage and identify their corresponding HTML code.
After the Learn stage, students move to the 
Practice module
.
（展示
 Practice 
模組、程式碼編輯區和練習任務的截圖。
）
In this module, students complete three short activities: editing a heading and paragraph, adding an image with alternative text, and repairing a broken heading and link.
For students who get stuck, we included a 
Progressive Hints
 system.
（展示三層提示的設計示意或截圖。
）
✂ The first level gives a general direction, the second gives more specific structural guidance, and the last shows an example.
This feature is informed by 
Thomann and Deutscher (2025)
, whose meta-analysis found that appropriately designed prompts can support learning in digital environments, although the effectiveness varies between contexts.
Our aim is to help without removing the need to think, so the classroom rule is: check your work first, then take one hint. To be honest, the current version does not enforce this rule, so a student could click straight through to the example. The teacher therefore watches hint use while circulating.
The Practice module also gives basic automated feedback, for example when a required HTML element or attribute is missing.
These checks cover selected technical requirements only, and my groupmate will return to this limitation.
✂ Overall, the Learn and Practice modules help students move from recognising HTML elements to applying them in structured tasks.
Next, my groupmate will introduce the Build module, learner diversity and classroom management.
Presenter 4 — Independent Creation, Learner Diversity and Classroom Management
Thank you.
The final module, 
Build
, lets students apply their HTML knowledge to an authentic task: creating a personal portfolio webpage.
（展示
 Build 
模組的截圖，包括
 Starter Template
、
HTML Editor
、
Live Preview 
和
 Checklist
。）
Students are provided with a partially completed HTML template.
They write their own heading and introduction, describe the image with alternative text and make the hyperlink work.
✂ A live preview shows the effect of each change, and students export their HTML files before the stage ends.
We provide a starter template because students are writing HTML for the first time and Build lasts only eighteen minutes.
But a completed template does not prove understanding, so each student also explains one HTML choice to a partner.
For 
learner diversity
, students who need help use the starter template, worked examples and progressive hints, and students who finish early attempt extension tasks.
✂ And because a portfolio is about the student, each learner brings their own interests, background and language to the page. Using students' backgrounds and experiences in this way is a core idea of culturally responsive teaching (
Chuang et al., 2020
).
For 
positive classroom management
, we organise our strategies into three stages: 
Prevent, Support and Promote
.
（展示
 Overview
：
Prevent → Support → Promote
。）
Each stage responds to one problem that is likely in a computer-room lesson like ours.
（展示情境
 1
：
Student Distraction
。）
The first problem is 
student distraction
: during Build, some students open YouTube or online games instead of working on their webpage.
In a computer room, distraction is always one click away, and the teacher cannot watch every screen.
So we begin with 
preventive classroom management
 (
Oliver, 2014
): setting expectations before the problem appears, rather than waiting until students are distracted.
Before Practice begins, the teacher states one clear rule: only Portfolio Lab should be open during Practice and Build.
We also give 
tight instructions
. The eighteen minutes of Build are divided into short stages with time limits:
six minutes for the heading and introduction, five minutes for the image and its alternative text, five minutes for the hyperlink and a final check of the preview, and the last two minutes for the export.
The rule and the time limits stay on the screen, because instructions should also be available in writing (
Bluestein, 2008
).
At the end of each stage, the teacher circulates to check progress and makes sure everyone knows the next step.
Clear expectations tell students what to do and when to finish, and short time limits leave little empty time for them to drift away.
✂ If a student still goes off task, the teacher first moves closer with a non-verbal cue, then privately names the current step, for example, "You are on step 2: add the alt text." A student who has already finished gets an extension task.
✂ This is also why prevention works better than punishment alone: punishment comes after learning time is lost, and closing YouTube does not tell a student which step to do next.
（展示情境
 2
：
Refusal to Participate
。）
The second problem is 
refusal to participate
.
During Practice, a student keeps failing to repair a heading with a missing closing tag, becomes frustrated, stops working and says, "I can't do this."
One possible explanation is Dreikurs' idea of 
assumed inadequacy
, from our Session 6 (
Cheung, 2026
): the student believes they cannot succeed, so they withdraw to avoid further failure.
But this is only one possible explanation. Before responding, the teacher asks what skill the student is not yet showing (
Hue & Li, 2008
).
Our key point is that "I can't do this" is first a signal of learning difficulty, not a discipline problem.
✂ By this point, the student has usually gone through the 
help routine
 on the screen: check the preview, check the hints and ask a partner. Now it is the teacher's turn.
So the teacher uses 
Private Individual Correction
: walking over and quietly asking, "Which part is not working?", instead of criticising the student in front of the class.
✂ This protects the student's dignity and avoids unnecessary confrontation.
Then, to build a 
positive student–teacher relationship
, the teacher gives a smaller step first, such as changing only the text inside the heading, and then points the student to one progressive hint for the missing closing tag.
When the student succeeds, the teacher gives specific encouragement, such as "Good, your heading now shows correctly," and then increases the difficulty step by step.
（展示情境
 3
：
Unequal Participation
。）
The third problem is 
unequal participation
.
In paired activities, such as the HTML X-Ray activity, one student may take over the mouse and keyboard while the partner just watches.
Putting students together does not automatically create cooperative learning. Without clear roles, the stronger student does everything and the other free-rides.
So we use 
group-oriented classroom management
. Partners take two clear roles: a 
Code Editor
, who uses the mouse and keyboard, and a 
Reviewer
, who checks the preview and explains the element.
They swap roles after each element until each partner has explained at least three elements, so both students get hands-on practice.
✂ Pairs use the same editing and checking roles if a computer fails, as my groupmate will explain.
Before pair sharing, the teacher also sets the feedback rule: 
one strength and one specific suggestion
.
The teacher then gives 
behaviour-specific praise
, for example: "I noticed that you both took turns and explained each element to each other."
Students then know exactly which behaviour the teacher values, and other pairs can see a model.
To sum up, Prevent reduces distraction, Support makes sure struggling students are not left behind, and Promote encourages everyone to take part.
✂ Prevention is our first choice, but clear and fair consequences are still needed if a behaviour continues.
Finally, for privacy, students may use nicknames, fictional identities and appropriately licensed images, and they are not required to publish their websites.
Next, my groupmate will explain our assessment design, contingency arrangements and critical reflection.
Presenter 5 — Assessment, Contingency Plan, Critical Reflection and Conclusion
Thank you.
An important question in our design is: how can we know whether students have actually learned HTML?
A student may produce a working webpage from a template without understanding the code, so we use several forms of assessment.
At the end of the lesson, students share in pairs and complete an individual 
Exit Ticket
.
（展示
 Exit Ticket 
樣本及
 Learning Objectives–Assessment Alignment 
表格。）
The Exit Ticket asks students to explain two HTML elements, to repair a link with a missing 
href
 attribute, and to describe an error they fixed and how they tested it. Each question is aligned with a learning objective.
According to 
Black and Wiliam (2009)
, assessment becomes formative when teachers, learners or their peers use the evidence to decide the next steps.
In our lesson all three do: students act on the automated checks, partners give feedback, and the teacher adjusts the next lesson.
For example, students submit their downloaded learning records after class. If several confuse the 
src
 and 
href
 attributes, the teacher revisits them at the start of the next lesson.
However, the automated checks only cover selected technical requirements.
They cannot show whether a student understands the code independently, so we combine them with teacher observation, peer explanation and the individual Exit Ticket.
Because the lesson relies on computers, our 
Contingency Plan
 has three levels.
（展示
 Offline Portfolio Lab
、
Student Worksheets 
和
 Teacher Answers 
的封面或截圖。）
If the network or platform fails, students use the offline version of Portfolio Lab.
If one computer fails, students pair up and take turns editing and checking.
If no computers work, the teacher distributes printed worksheets with the same diagnostic questions, debugging tasks, a paper portfolio and the Exit Ticket.
Paper cannot replace browser testing, so students complete the technical checks once the equipment returns.
Finally, we reflect critically on three limitations.
The first is time. Students have only eighteen minutes for independent creation, and the final ten minutes are even tighter, so we limit the minimum requirement to a basic HTML draft.
The second is a trade-off in our own design.
To protect privacy, Portfolio Lab does not collect students' answers. So the teacher has no live class data and must rely on circulation, quick polls and the records submitted after class.
The third is that Portfolio Lab has not been evaluated with real students.
Its design is informed by research, but we cannot claim that it improves learning outcomes.
✂ In a real classroom, we would collect students' work, common errors and Exit Tickets, and use them to improve the materials.
To conclude, our plan helps students progress from recognising HTML elements to creating their own webpages.
Through 
Gagné's Nine Events
, 
prior knowledge activation
, 
scaffolding
, 
formative assessment
 and 
positive classroom management
, we aim to support active engagement and independent learning.
More importantly, students see how ICT knowledge connects with a practical skill: organising and presenting their work to others.
Thank you for listening to our presentation.
We welcome your questions.
